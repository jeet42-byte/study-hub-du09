1. How to answer scenario questions

Every question gives you a short story — a raid, a hacked account, a seized phone — and then asks 2–3 questions about it. The examiner wants to see that you can apply the concepts, not just recite them.

A 5-step answer recipe: (1) name the issue in the scenario ("this is a mobile-evidence problem"); (2) define the concept from the slides; (3) list the steps or features, numbered; (4) apply each one to the scenario (who, what device, which tool); (5) finish with the legal/ethical point — chain of custody, legal authority — and a one-line conclusion.

Almost every scenario can be helped by the same backbone, so learn it well:

Secure & isolate→Document→Capture volatile data→Image with write blocker→Hash→Analyse the copy→Report

2. What digital forensics is

Digital forensics is the process of investigating digital devices for evidence — collecting, preserving and analysing digital evidence — used in criminal, civil and corporate investigations. Its key goal is to identify, recover and document evidence.

It is crime-scene investigation for computers and phones. Just as a detective collects fingerprints without smudging them, a digital investigator collects files, chats and logs without changing a single bit — and can prove it.

Why it matters

  • Helps police solve cybercrimes
  • Identifies cyber threats and security breaches
  • Essential for corporate and legal investigations
  • Ensures digital evidence keeps its integrity in court

The six-stage process

Identification→Collection→Preservation→Analysis→Documentation→Presentation
StagePlain meaning
IdentificationFind which devices, accounts and data might hold evidence
CollectionSeize devices or get the data lawfully
PreservationProtect it from any change — write blockers, hashes, Faraday bags, custody forms
AnalysisSearch the copy for relevant evidence with forensic tools
DocumentationRecord every step, tool and result
PresentationExplain the findings in a report and in court

Real-world uses

Cybercrime (fraud, hacking, identity theft) · corporate security and insider-threat detection · incident response to breaches · intellectual-property theft and data leaks · terrorism and organized crime.

The 2016 Bangladesh Bank cyber heist: attackers used malware and the bank's SWIFT access to send fraudulent payment orders worth about USD 951 million; USD 81 million reached accounts in the Philippines. Understanding how it happened depended on forensic examination of the bank's systems.

3. Digital evidence

Digital evidence is information stored or transmitted in binary form that may be relied on in court. It supports criminal, civil and administrative cases, can rebuild timelines and reveal user behaviour, and often needs special tools to extract and read.

Types

Computer files · network logs and internet history · cloud-stored information · emails and messaging logs · mobile device data · memory dumps and system artifacts.

Four characteristics (very likely to be asked)

CharacteristicMeaningSo you must…
FragileEasily changed or destroyed — even by opening a fileNever browse the original; use write blockers
VolatileSome data vanishes when power is cut (RAM, live sessions)Capture it first, before switching off
HiddenNot visible to normal users (deleted files, metadata, logs)Use forensic tools to find it
DuplicableCan be copied exactlyMake an exact image and work on the copy

Sources

PCs and laptops · mobile devices and wearables · IoT devices and surveillance systems · external storage (USB, SSD) · network devices · cloud environments and email servers.

Evidence is everywhere a person's digital life touches: the phone in their pocket, the watch on their wrist, the CCTV on the street, the router at home and the cloud far away.

4. Chain of custody, law and ethics

Chain of custody is the documented, unbroken record of who handled each item of evidence, when, where and why — from seizure to court. The lecture calls it "very crucial to ensure that evidence is not tampered with".

Think of it as a signed travel diary for each phone or laptop. If a page is missing — nobody knows who had the phone on Tuesday — the defence can claim someone changed it.
Seizure→Label & bag→Evidence register→Lab: image + hash→Analysis on copy→Report→Court

Four legal and ethical principles

PrincipleMeaning
Chain of custodyDocument every step from collection to analysis
Legal authority & privacyNo access to personal data without legal authorization, even if useful; comply with privacy laws (the slides cite GDPR, HIPAA)
ImpartialityNever manipulate evidence to fit a story; report what helps the accused too
ProportionalityCollect and analyse only what the case needs
Logging into a suspect's account with a password found in his diary is unauthorized access — even for police — unless legally authorized. Use legal requests to the provider instead.

5. The first responder and the digital crime scene

"As a first responder, you are the gatekeeper of digital truth." What you do in the first minutes can preserve evidence — or destroy it forever.

First responder duties

  • Secure the scene; stop anyone touching devices
  • Photograph and document device condition
  • Record time, people present and actions taken
  • Preserve the digital state (on stays on, off stays off)

Securing the scene

ActionWhy
Disconnect from networks (unplug Ethernet, disable Wi-Fi)Stop remote commands and data theft
Check for active connections, VPNs, remote desktop, syncThese can trigger remote wiping
Phones into Faraday bagsBlock Wi-Fi, cellular and Bluetooth — no remote wipe or tracking
Label and isolate every device; keep logsStarts the chain of custody

What to document

Make, model, serial numbers · connected ports and peripherals · system status (on/off, logged in/out) with timestamps and screen photos · visible signs of tampering.

Seizing

  • Use write blockers for storage devices
  • Label and log every device with a unique ID
  • Use anti-static, tamper-evident evidence bags
  • Don't power on a device that is off — it may change data or run destructive scripts

Volatile and network evidence

  • If a device is on, capture RAM and active sessions first (tools: Volatility, FTK Imager)
  • Record running processes, open files, sessions, ports and remote IPs
  • Collect logs from routers, firewalls and servers; capture traffic with Wireshark; map IPs and timestamps; export logs in their original format

Common mistakes

Failing to document · powering on suspect devices · not isolating devices from networks · using unverified tools · mishandling the chain of custody.

The lecture case study: in an insider-theft case, the responder failed to isolate a laptop and phone; the VPN auto-connected, a remote wipe was triggered and critical data was lost. Lesson: isolate immediately.

6. Imaging, hashing, live vs static acquisition

Disk imaging

The cornerstone of forensics: an exact, bit-for-bit copy of a drive, including deleted data. Always use a write blocker, hash before and after, keep the original and working copies separately, and analyse only the copy. Tools: FTK Imager, EnCase, Guymager, dd.

A write blocker is a one-way valve: the computer can read the suspect's drive but cannot write a single byte to it.

Hashing

A hash (MD5, SHA-1, SHA-256) is a digital fingerprint of data. Change one bit and the fingerprint changes completely. If the hash of the copy matches the hash of the original, the copy is identical — mathematical proof for the court. Document every hash and verify at every step.

Like a seal on a sealed envelope: if the seal (hash) is the same when it reaches court, nobody opened it.

Live vs static acquisition

Live acquisitionStatic acquisition
WhenSystem is ONAfter power-off
CapturesRAM, active sessions, running malwareWhole disk, with no changes during imaging
ToolsBelkasoft / Magnet RAM CaptureFTK Imager, Guymager, EnCase
If the computer is running, do live first, static second — once you switch it off, RAM (and any memory-only malware or encryption keys) is gone.

7. Analysing the evidence

TechniqueWhat it doesTools
File system analysisRebuilds folders, recovers deleted files, reads metadata (last access, owner, permissions)Autopsy, Sleuth Kit, X-Ways
Data carvingFinds files by their signature headers (JPG, PDF) without the file table — works on formatted or damaged drivesPhotoRec, Scalpel, Foremost
Memory forensicsAnalyses RAM for running processes, passwords, keys and malware that lives only in memoryVolatility, Rekall (capture: FTK Imager, Belkasoft)
Registry artifactsShow USB devices connected (make, serial, time), MAC addresses and moreEnCase, X-Ways
Email & browser artifactsCached emails and attachments, browsing history, cookies, autofillInternet Evidence Finder, MailXaminer, X-Ways
Timeline analysisCombines events from all sources into one storylog2timeline / Plaso, X-Ways Trace
Deleting a file usually just removes its "address label"; the content stays until something writes over it. File-system analysis finds the label; carving finds the content even when the labels are gone.
Lecture case study: an executive suspected of leaking files. FTK Imager cloned the drive; Autopsy showed suspicious document access; Magnet AXIOM recovered browser evidence of files sent by webmail; the timeline proved it happened after business hours.

8. Reporting, tools and their limits

The report

Record every tool used with its version number and every step; include hashes, screenshots, timelines and chain-of-custody logs; make it clear, professional and admissible. "Your report is your final product… It should withstand cross-examination."

Choosing and validating tools

Use forensically sound, court-admissible tools · verify them before use · match them to the evidence type · document version and configuration.

Best practices

Work on copies, not originals · validate tools before critical cases · stay updated · cross-check results with more than one tool.

Limitations

May miss encrypted data or steganography · user errors or unsupported file systems leave gaps · newest devices/OS may not be supported · bugs can give false results.

9. Mobile forensics

Mobile forensics is recovering digital evidence from phones, tablets and wearables using forensically sound methods. Phones often hold more case-relevant personal data than computers: calls, texts, app logs, photos, GPS.

Objectives: data recovery · evidence preservation · activity reconstruction (where, which apps, who) · investigative support.

Challenges: device diversity (Android across many brands) · encryption (Android full-disk encryption, iOS Secure Enclave) · rapid updates · cloud integration.

Four types / acquisition methods

TypeWhat it getsPlain words
ManualScreenshots of what's on screenPhotographing the phone screen — last resort
LogicalUser data (contacts, SMS, app data) via backups or APIsCopying what the user can see
File systemSpecific partitions (e.g. /data) incl. app databasesOpening the app's filing cabinets
PhysicalBit-by-bit memory image incl. deleted data; may need chip-offCopying everything, even the "rubbish bin"
CloudSynced backups and photos via credentials or legal requestFetching the copy kept online
Identification (device & OS)→Seizure (isolate)→Acquisition (extract)→Analysis→Reporting

Seizure best practices

Airplane mode or Faraday bag · gloves (fingerprints/DNA) · record state (on, locked, battery) · photograph device, cables, SIM.

Eight ways to isolate a phone from the network

EMI-shielded room/enclosure · airplane/disabled mode · no SIM or a forensic/cloned SIM · RF interference device or jammer · StrongHold box · Faraday bags and cages · aluminium foil · ask the operator to disable the account. Note: isolation drains the battery fast.

Identify the device

Form factor (bar, flip, clamshell, twister, spinner, slider) · condition · make and model · IMEI / ESN / MEID · serial number · ICCID of the SIM · (PIN for BlackBerry only).

Battery rules

Don't remove the battery (volatile data loss) · most acquisitions need more than 50% charge · some phones shut down below a threshold · a small charge can hold volatile data briefly.

Tools

Cellebrite UFED (industry standard) · Oxygen Forensics (cloud, decrypting WhatsApp/Telegram data) · Magnet AXIOM (device + cloud) · XRY (MSAB).

10. Cloud forensics

Cloud computing is delivering servers, storage, databases, networking and software over the internet.

ModelExampleWhat evidence the customer controls
IaaSRented virtual serversMost (VMs, OS logs)
PaaSApp platformsApps and their logs
SaaSGmail, Google DriveLeast — the provider holds it

Deployment: public, private, hybrid, community.

Five challenges

ChallengePlain words
Data locationData sits in other countries
Multi-tenancyMany customers share the same machines — you can't seize the server
Lack of controlYou depend on the provider
Data volatilityAuto-scaling creates and deletes machines constantly
EncryptionNo keys, no access

Getting cloud evidence lawfully

Warrants, subpoenas or MLATs (mutual legal assistance treaties) · work with providers for access logs (login data, IP history) · extract files with their metadata and timestamps · document every request and step.

Identification→Preservation (snapshots, logs)→Analysis (correlate VMs, storage, logs)→Presentation

Tools: AWS CloudTrail (API and user activity) · Azure Monitor / Sentinel · Google Cloud Logging · Splunk, EnCase, FTK Imager.

In the cloud, you usually can't grab the hard drive — you have to ask the company that owns it, through the law. That's why cloud cases are slower and depend on paperwork.

11. IoT forensics

IoT is a network of connected devices exchanging data — smart thermostats, wearables, sensors, CCTV, smart locks. Characteristics: massive scale · real-time data · limited resources.

Challenges: device diversity · limited storage (data overwritten quickly) · proprietary protocols · resource constraints · chain of custody across devices.

Acquiring IoT evidence

  1. Identify connected devices (cameras, speakers, doorbells, thermostats)
  2. Preserve configuration, onboard storage and linked cloud accounts
  3. Extract logs, footage and interaction data (motion events, commands)
  4. Document network paths and paired devices
Identification→Preservation (devices, cloud, gateways)→Analysis (timestamps)→Presentation (timeline)

Tools: Wireshark, Magnet AXIOM, Autopsy, Cellebrite UFED.

12. Email and social media forensics

Collecting, preserving and analysing evidence from email and social media using forensically sound methods — email clients, webmail, Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp.

Objectives: evidence recovery · preservation · behaviour analysis (relationships, timelines) · investigative support (fraud, defamation, data leaks).

Challenges: huge data volume · end-to-end encryption (WhatsApp, Signal) · cloud storage needs legal access · ephemeral content (disappearing messages, deleted posts).

TypeFocus
Email forensicsHeaders, content, attachments, server logs
Social media forensicsPosts, comments, DMs, metadata
Cloud-basedGmail, social media backups
Device-basedApp data and cache on phones/computers
Identification→Acquisition→Preservation→Analysis→Reporting

Acquisition

  • Email: local (.pst files) · cloud (webmail via credentials/legal request) · server SMTP/IMAP logs · tools Magnet AXIOM, MailXaminer (headers)
  • Social media: device-based (WhatsApp SQLite databases) · cloud-based (platform APIs, Facebook data download, legal requests) · web scraping of public posts if lawful · tools Cellebrite, Oxygen
  • Preservation: Faraday bags · provider backups (e.g. Google Takeout) · screenshots of ephemeral content · MD5/SHA hashing
End-to-end encryption locks messages while they travel, not on the phone. That's why the seized phone — not WhatsApp's server — is where investigators find the chats.

13. Challenges, new technology and best practice

General challenges: encryption · cloud and remote storage · huge volumes · fast-changing technology · anti-forensics (wiping, secure deletion, hiding data).

Emerging technologyEffect

BlockchainTamper-proof evidence records
Edge computingFaster IoT analysis
Quantum computingThreatens today's encryption

Future trends: more automation · standard IoT protocols · zero trust · cross-border collaboration · privacy-preserving forensics (homomorphic encryption).

Best practices for organizations: proactive monitoring (enable logging and auditing) · regular backups · training · agreements with cloud providers · documentation.

17. Last-minute revision sheet

  1. Digital forensics = investigating digital devices to identify, recover and document evidence. Process: identification → collection → preservation → analysis → documentation → presentation.
  2. Digital evidence = binary information that may be relied on in court; fragile, volatile, hidden, duplicable.
  3. Chain of custody = signed record of every hand-over; plus legal authority, impartiality, proportionality.
  4. First responder: secure, isolate (Faraday bag, unplug network, check VPN), document, don't power on, capture RAM first.
  5. Imaging = bit-for-bit copy through a write blocker; hash (SHA-256) before and after; analyse the copy.
  6. Live (on, RAM) before static (off, disk).
  7. Analysis: file system (deleted files, metadata) · carving (signatures, formatted drives) · memory (Volatility) · registry USB artifacts · browser/email artifacts · timeline (Plaso).
  8. Mobile: manual / logical / file system / physical (chip-off) / cloud; isolate (8 methods); IMEI + ICCID; keep battery >50%; Cellebrite, Oxygen, AXIOM, XRY.
  9. Cloud: IaaS/PaaS/SaaS; challenges — location, multi-tenancy, control, volatility, encryption; MLAT; CloudTrail.
  10. IoT: scale, real-time, limited resources; challenges — diversity, storage, protocols, resources, custody.
  11. Email/social: headers + SMTP/IMAP logs; WhatsApp SQLite on the device; E2EE and ephemeral content are the challenges.
  12. Report: tools + versions, steps, hashes, screenshots, timeline, custody log — must survive cross-examination.
