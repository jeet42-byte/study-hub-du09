1. How to use these notes

The final is 50 marks: answer 5 of 7–8 questions, 10 marks each. Every question has 3 parts worth 3–4 marks. A typical question looks like: (a) define X [3], (b) explain or compare its types [4], (c) apply it to an example or calculate [3].

Recipe for every part: 1) the slide definition, in her words → 2) the list or table (types, steps, pros and cons) → 3) one example from criminology, ideally from Bangladesh → 4) one line of evaluation. For calculations: sort → formula → answer with units → comment on outliers.

Her finals cover the whole course, one chapter at a time (see section 2). With 7–8 questions over 7–9 chapters, expect about one question per chapter. You cannot skip a chapter completely — but you can choose which 5 to answer.

3. Chapter 1 — Research, terms and theories

What research is

Research is a systematic process of gathering, analysing and interpreting information to establish facts and reach new conclusions. It is a structured way of investigating a problem, phenomenon or theory to expand knowledge.

Research is "finding out properly" — following clear steps so that someone else could check your work and reach the same answer.

Why research matters in the social sciences: it gives evidence to understand social problems, influence policy and find solutions; it advances knowledge; it helps us understand social reality empirically; it lets decisions rest on evidence; and it supports societal development by explaining human behaviour.

Basic researchApplied research
Gains fundamental knowledge (why do people desist from crime?)Solves a specific problem (does this CCTV project reduce snatching?)
QualitativeQuantitative
In-depth understanding, in wordsNumerical data, measurement and statistics

"Live objectively": researchers observe, they don't judge; they stay open to all ideas; they detach from their own views so they can empathise. Maxfield & Babbie list the everyday errors this protects against: inaccurate observation, overgeneralisation, selective observation, illogical reasoning, ideology and politics. The four purposes of research are exploration, description, explanation and application.

The six terms

TermMeaningExample
ConceptAn abstract idea or general notion representing a phenomenonJustice, poverty, fear of crime
VariableA measurable representation of a concept that can varyMonthly income (for poverty)
FactA statement objectively verifiable by observation or evidenceWater boils at 100°C at sea level
TheoryA well-substantiated explanation based on evidence that has stood up to repeated testingSocial learning theory
PropositionA statement of judgement about the relationship between conceptsParental involvement improves school performance
HypothesisA specific, testable prediction about the relationship between variablesHigher stress → lower academic performance

Variables: the independent variable is the presumed cause (education); the dependent variable is the outcome (income); a control variable is held constant (age). Hypotheses: H₀ (null) says there is no relationship; H₁ (alternative) says there is one.

Concept = the idea. Variable = the idea made measurable. Hypothesis = your bet about how two variables are linked. Theory = a bet that has already won many times.

Ten characteristics of a good social-science theory

  1. Empirical foundation — based on observation and evidence.
  2. Systematic and organised — a logical framework of concepts.
  3. Explanatory and predictive power — explains why, and sometimes predicts.
  4. Testable — produces hypotheses that can be confirmed or refuted.
  5. Abstract and generalisable — applies across contexts (though not always universally).
  6. Provisional and evolving — revised or replaced as evidence changes.
  7. Value-informed — may reflect perspectives (Marxist, feminist, postcolonial).
  8. Contextually sensitive — historical, cultural, political and economic context.
  9. Multidimensional — many variables and intersections (race, class, gender).
  10. Practically relevant — informs policy and social change.
Memory hook: "Every System Explains, Tests, Abstracts, Provisionally — Values, Context, Multiple dimensions, Practice."

4. Chapter 2 — Qualitative research, grounded theory and coding

Why qualitative research matters

It gives an in-depth understanding of behaviour, perspectives and experiences; it generates knowledge beyond numerical frameworks; and it gives insight into social phenomena. The major methods are grounded theory, ethnography, phenomenology, case study and narrative research.

Grounded theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1960s; The Discovery of Grounded Theory, 1967)

Develops theory from the data, so the theory is "grounded" in observation. They saw the need because qualitative research needed: independence from existing theories, more rigour, an inductive approach, rejection of the "hypothesis-first" model, and a focus on generating theory, not just testing it.

Process: a complex, iterative cycle. Start with open "generative questions" → identify core concepts as data come in → develop tentative links between concepts and data. The supporting chapter adds constant comparison, theoretical sampling (the emerging theory decides whom to study next) and memos; Bryman & Burgess add saturation (stop when new data add nothing).

Ordinary research: guess first, then check. Grounded theory: look first, and let the explanation grow out of what you see.

The three analytical strategies

StrategyWhat it is
CodingCategorising the data and describing what the categories mean. Open coding first (minute detail, initial categories); then selective coding around one core concept. Strauss & Corbin put axial coding in between (linking categories).
MemoingRecording the researcher's thoughts as they evolve — extensive margin notes; open at first, focused on the core concept later.
Integrative diagrams and sessionsAny graphic that helps build the theory; group sessions where team members share ideas.

6. Chapter 4 — Statistics and sampling

Statistics in social research is the scientific method for collecting, organising, analysing, interpreting and presenting data to draw conclusions about a population from a sample. Population = everyone you are interested in; sample = the smaller group that represents them; sampling = how you choose the sample.

You can't interview all 3,000 inmates, so you pick 300 in a fair way and use them to speak for the rest.
Probability (random)Non-probability
SelectionRandomisation; everyone has a known, non-zero chanceDeliberate choice — convenience, geography, cost
Generalise?Yes, statisticallyNo
MethodsSimple random, systematic, stratified, clusterConvenience, purposive, snowball, quota

Probability methods

MethodHowProCon

Systematic example: N = 600 inmates, n = 60 → k = 600 ÷ 60 = 10 Random start between 1 and 10 → say 7 Sample: 7, 17, 27, 37 … 597 (60 inmates) Risk: if every 10th name on the list is a ward leader → biased. Shuffle the list first.
Stratified (proportional) example: 2,000 students, sample 200 (10%) Criminology 600 → 60 · Sociology 500 → 50 · Anthropology 400 → 40 · Others 500 → 50

Trap: quota sampling looks like stratified sampling — both use groups — but quota is non-random inside each group. Stratified = probability; quota = non-probability.

7. Chapter 5 — Surveys, self-reports and questionnaires

Survey research applies a standard instrument in a systematic way to take measures from a large number of units. It is useful for qualitative and quantitative, descriptive, explanatory, exploratory and applied research — best where people are the units (respondents or informants). Examples: the NCVS and the UCR.

Surveys in criminology

TypeWhat it measuresExample
Self-reportsCrimes respondents themselves committed — frequency and prevalenceChaiken & Chaiken (1982)
Perceptions and attitudesOpinions — fear of crime, trust in policeDhaka residents' trust in police
Targeted victim surveysA selected group of victimsGarment workers and harassment
General-purpose crime surveysNational crime statisticsBritish Crime Survey: fear of crime (1984), police contacts (1988, 1996, 2000), drug use (1994), security (1996), fraud and technology (2002–03)
Police records only count crimes someone reported. A survey asks people directly, so it finds the hidden "dark figure" too.

Varieties of Criminal Behavior (Chaiken & Chaiken, 1982)

  • Rand Corporation; nearly 2,200 male prison and jail inmates in California, Michigan and Texas; a self-report questionnaire plus official records.
  • Offenders sorted into 10 types by their combination of crimes; the most serious were the "violent predators", who commit robbery, assault and drug dealing at high rates.
  • Violent predators were young (under 23 on average), violent before age 16, and heavy hard-drug users from their juvenile years, with unstable jobs.
  • Official records could not tell them apart from lesser offenders — self-reports could.
  • Validity checks: paired questions (over 83% consistent); comparison with records (59% had under 20% error); analysis repeated without the 42% of doubtful cases (same results).

Questionnaires

A "collection of questions". Good items are clear, short, not negative, not biased or leading, and ask one thing at a time (no double-barrelled items).

BadWhyBetter
"Are the police honest and efficient?"Double-barrelledAsk about honesty and efficiency separately
"Police should not search without a warrant."Negative"Police should need a warrant to search."
"Don't you agree corrupt officers should be punished?"Leading"Should officers convicted of bribery be prosecuted?"

Format: uncluttered with reading space; contingency questions (asked only if a filter answer applies — "If YES, did you report it?"); matrix questions (several items with the same answer scale in a grid); order from general to specific, avoiding biased sequences.

Self-administered: mail surveys are still widely used — convenient, easy to distribute. Use a cover letter from an institution respondents respect or a neutral, impressive affiliation. Emphasise the response rate (rule of thumb: 50% adequate, 60% good, 70% very good). Computer-based surveys are cheaper and more accessible.

A key informant interview is an in-depth interview with someone who has special knowledge, position or access (like Whyte's Doc). It gives insight and access, but the informant's bias can shape the picture. For interview types and FGDs, see Chapter 3 above.

The mean is like a see-saw — one very heavy person (an outlier) tips it. The median is the person standing in the middle of a queue — it doesn't matter how tall the last person is.

9. Chapter 7 — Content analysis

Content analysis is a research method that uses a set of procedures to make valid inferences from text.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Works directly with transcripts of human communication
Both qualitative and quantitative
Covers different time periods
Relatively less data bias		Time-consuming and labour-intensive
Subjectivity and researcher bias
Loss of context and nuance
Focus on surface (manifest) content
Hard to establish causality

Manifest vs latent: manifest = the visible words; latent = the deeper meaning. Ring a ring o' roses: manifest = a children's game; popular latent reading = the plague (rash, posies, sneezing, death). Folklorists doubt it, because the rhyme appeared in print centuries later — a lesson about the risk of over-reading latent meaning.

Reliability (Krippendorff, 1980)Validity
Stability — same coder, same result over time
Reproducibility — intercoder reliability
Accuracy — matches a standard or norm		Face — the researcher gives meaning (subjective)
Predictive — compared with an external criterion
Semantic — language experts agree the meanings are consistent

Eight steps: 1 define recording units → 2 define categories → 3 test coding on a sample → 4 assess accuracy/reliability → 5 revise the coding rules → 6 go back to step 3 → 7 code all the text → 8 assess achieved reliability.

10. Ethics (Class 12 — if taught before the final)

  • No harm to participants — physical, psychological, legal, social.
  • Voluntary participation and informed consent — especially delicate with prisoners.
  • Anonymity (even the researcher can't identify the respondent) vs confidentiality (the researcher knows but promises not to tell).
  • Avoid deception unless essential; debrief afterwards.
  • Honest analysis and reporting; ethics review by an Institutional Review Board.
  • Famous cases: Stanford Prison Experiment (Zimbardo, 1971 — stopped after 6 days); Tearoom Trade (Humphreys, 1970 — covert observation, traced number plates).

11. Writing research reports (Classes 13–14 — if taught)

Structure: title and abstract → introduction → literature review → methodology → findings → discussion → conclusion and recommendations → references (APA) and appendices. Avoid plagiarism: quote with quotation marks and a page number, paraphrase properly and cite, keep a record of sources. Good tables and charts have a numbered title, labelled columns and axes, the sample size (n) and a source.

14. Exam traps

  • Mean for categorical data — never. Use the mode for categories and the median for ranks.
  • Even n — the median is the average of the two middle values (9 + 10) ÷ 2 = 9.5.
  • Sort first — the median and quartiles need ordered data.
  • Systematic — only the start is random; k = N ÷ n.
  • Snowball is for hidden populations; purposive is for chosen experts.
  • Grounded theory rejects the hypothesis-first approach — don't write "GT tests hypotheses".
  • Stability is one coder over time; reproducibility is several coders.
  • Double-barrelled, negative and leading are the three classic bad-question errors — name them.
