The topics most likely to appear (from the slides) are: ethnography and Street Corner Society, overt vs covert research, interview types, phenomenology and case studies. The book pages add participant observation, covert participation, and sampling.

2. What is qualitative research?

Qualitative methods are methods such as participant observation, intensive interviewing and focus groups that are designed to capture social life as participants experience it, rather than in categories the researcher decides in advance (Bachman & Schutt, p. 237).

Quantitative research counts ("How many people were robbed?"). Qualitative research understands ("What is it like to be robbed, and what does it mean to the victim?"). One gives numbers; the other gives stories, meanings and context.
Quantitative (surveys, experiments)Qualitative (observation, interviews)
DataNumbersWords, observations, stories
QuestionsFixed in advance (closed questions)Open, flexible, can change during the study
ReasoningDeductive — test a hypothesisInductive — discover patterns from the data
SampleLarge, often randomSmall, chosen on purpose
ResearcherTries to stay neutral and distantGets close; their own role is part of the study
StrengthGeneralizable, measurableDepth, meaning, context

The three main qualitative designs in the book are participant observation, intensive interviewing and focus groups (focus groups are not in this quiz).

A survey can tell us how many women in Dhaka feel unsafe on buses. Qualitative interviews can tell us why — what happens, how they cope, why they rarely report it.

3. The 8 features of qualitative methods

Bachman & Schutt list features that make qualitative research different from experiments and surveys (pp. 237–238):

#FeatureIn plain words
1Collection mainly of qualitative (not quantitative) dataWords and observations of real life, not numbers in boxes
2Exploratory questions + inductive reasoningStart with an open question, collect lots of observations, then build general ideas — not the other way round
3Focus on previously unstudied processes and unexpected thingsGood for new issues and hard-to-reach groups (gangs, drug users)
4Orientation to social contextLooks at how things connect in a setting — a neighbourhood, a gang, a police station (e.g. Venkatesh on Blackstone)
5Focus on human subjectivityWhat events mean to people and how they make sense of their own lives
6Focus on events leading up to an outcomeTells the story of how something happened, step by step, instead of "variable X causes Y"
7Reflexive research designThe plan changes as the study goes on; collecting, analysing and re-thinking happen together (Maxwell, 1996)
8Sensitivity to the subjective role of the researcherThe researcher admits they are not a neutral machine; their position affects what they see
"Testing a pre-written hypothesis in a controlled design" is not a feature of qualitative research — that describes experiments. Qualitative research is inductive, not deductive.

4. The qualitative research process (Exhibit 9.1)

Miller and Crabtree (1999) drew the qualitative process as a loop rather than a straight line:

Reflect on yourself & the setting→Sample / collect→Describe→Organize→Connect→Corroborate→Write the account
Unlike a survey, where you collect everything first and analyse later, qualitative researchers gather and analyse at the same time. Each day's notes shape what they look for the next day. That is why the design is called reflexive.
In Exhibit 9.1, the two processes that run together are the gathering process and the analysis process.

5. Where it came from: field research

Field research = research in which natural social processes are studied as they happen and left relatively undisturbed (p. 238).

  • Anthropologists started it: Franz Boas visited Native American villages in the American Northwest; Bronislaw Malinowski lived among New Guinea natives. They showed the value of knowing a community closely, instead of relying on second-hand reports.
  • Sociologists at the University of Chicago (many were former social workers and reformers) used these methods in the city, treating it as a "social science laboratory".
  • By the 1930s–1950s, researchers stressed direct participation in community life.
Field research means going to where things happen — the street, the slum, the police station — instead of bringing people into a lab or sending them a questionnaire.

6. Ethnography

Ethnography is a qualitative research method used to study cultures and communities by immersing oneself in the participants' natural environment to understand their behaviours, beliefs and practices. It uses in-depth observation, interviews and analysis to give a rich, detailed picture of a social setting from the perspective of those being studied (lecture slides).

Ethnography is "living with people to understand them from the inside". Instead of asking a gang member to fill in a form, the ethnographer spends months — even years — around the gang, watching, talking and slowly understanding how they see the world.

Three key words to remember: immersion (living in the setting), natural environment (no lab), participants' perspective (their view, not the researcher's).

A criminology student who spends six months with rickshaw pullers in a Dhaka slum, joining their daily routine to understand how police extortion affects them, is doing ethnography.

7. Street Corner Society — the "critical case"

Street Corner Society: The Social Structure of an Italian Slum by William Foote Whyte (published 1943) studied young men in a poor Italian neighbourhood in Boston that Whyte called "Cornerville".

MethodWhat Whyte didWhy it mattered
Overt participant observationHis identity as a researcher was not hiddenMore ethical; people knew what he was doing
Key informant strategyRelied on a local leader, "Doc", for access and rapportDoc opened doors Whyte could never open alone
Long-term immersionAbout four years; lived with an Italian family and learned ItalianBecame accepted as part of the community

Criticisms (the "flipside")

CriticismExplanation
Direct involvement in illegal activitiesWhyte took part in vote fraud (and racketeering) to gain insight — an ethical problem
Doc's influence on the workRelying on one informant can bias the story — Doc had a rivalry with "Chick", his local opponent, so Whyte may have seen things through Doc's eyes
Anonymity issuesPeople in the community could be identified from the book
Whyte got amazing inside information because he lived there for years and had a powerful friend (Doc). But the same closeness caused problems: he broke the law, he may have taken Doc's side, and his subjects could be recognized.
Four years = Whyte. Eighteen months = Venkatesh (logging gang activities) and Susan Miller (with the police). Doc = Whyte's key informant; Chick = Doc's rival, not an informant.

8. Overt vs covert ethnography

Overt ethnography (overt participation)Covert ethnography (covert participation)
MeaningResearcher openly declares their role and intentionsResearcher does not reveal they are a researcher
AdvantagesMore ethical; easier communication (can ask questions, take notes openly)More access, because people act naturally and closed groups let you in
DisadvantagesMay change participants' behaviour (the reactive effect)Seen as less ethical; very stressful for the researcher
ExampleWhyte in CornervilleHumphreys as "watch queen"; Alfred in a Satanist group
Overt = "Hello, I'm a researcher." Covert = pretending to be just another member. Overt is honest but people may behave differently in front of you. Covert gets the real behaviour but involves deceiving people.
Studying an online extremist group by joining it as an ordinary member, without telling anyone you are a researcher, would be covert. Interviewing police officers after introducing yourself as a student researcher is overt.

9. Participant observation and the observational continuum

Participant observation = field research in which the researcher develops a sustained and intensive relationship with people while they go about their normal activities (p. 237). It is the main qualitative method — "fieldwork" in anthropology.

The researcher can take different roles along a continuum (Exhibit 9.2):

Complete observer↔Participant and observer↔Covert participant
RoleParticipates?Identity known?Example
Complete observerNoYes — publicly a researcherSusan Miller (1999) shadowing neighbourhood and patrol police officers to study community policing
Participant and observerYesYes (at least to some members)Whyte (1943); Venkatesh hanging out with the Black Kings gang
Covert participant (complete participant)Yes, fullyNo — hiddenHumphreys (1970), Alfred (1976), Goffman (1961)

The reactive effect

Reactive effect = changes in individual or group behaviour caused by being observed or studied (p. 242).

People act differently when they know they are being watched — like students behaving perfectly when the principal visits the class. A complete observer faces this problem most.

How to reduce it: spend a long time in the setting so people get used to you. Susan Miller spent 18 months with the police, became a "familiar face", and officers joked with her in the coffee shop. The effect is stronger in small groups or when the observer looks very different from the people being studied.

Choosing a role

The choice depends on the setting, the researcher's background and personality, the political situation and ethics — and it often changes during a project.

Venkatesh's lesson

Sudhir Venkatesh began with a survey, but gang members told him he was "not going to learn shit by asking these questions" and that he should "hang out with them". He switched to participant observation — playing racquetball, eating dinner with families — and discovered gang members were also schoolchildren, fathers and churchgoers.

Ethical advantages of being open (disclosure)

  • Participants can choose what to keep hidden.
  • The researcher can refuse to join unethical or dangerous activities without blowing their cover.
"Complete observation" (watching openly, not participating) and "complete participation" (= covert participation) are opposite ends of the continuum. Don't mix them up!

10. Covert participation: problems and ethics

Covert (complete) participation = the researcher does not reveal their identity and acts like an ordinary member, adopting the role of a "complete participant" (p. 244).

ResearcherCovert role
Laud Humphreys (1970)Acted as a "watch queen" (lookout) to study men's sexual behaviour in public restrooms
Randall Alfred (1976)Joined a group of Satanists; the leader later said he had always found Alfred "strange"
Erving Goffman (1961)Worked as a state hospital assistant to study treatment of psychiatric patients

Four problems of covert participants

  1. Cannot take notes openly or use recording devices — must write from memory later.
  2. Cannot ask questions that arouse suspicion — so they may not understand what things mean.
  3. The role is hard to play — they don't know how real members act in every situation; acting "wrong" can cause reactive effects anyway (Alfred).
  4. Must keep up the act all the time — huge psychological stress, especially if asked to take sides or commit crimes.

Going native = becoming so absorbed in the role that the researcher adopts the group's views and goals, abandons the research and stops thinking critically.

Covert research is like being an undercover agent: you can't write notes in public, you can't ask too many questions, one wrong move exposes you, and if you stay too long you might start to become one of them.

The ethics debate

PositionArgument
Against — Erikson (1967)Covert research is unethical by its very nature and should only be allowed in public settings; researchers can't predict harmful consequences, and discovery damages trust in social science
For (sometimes) — Punch (1994)A total ban would "kill many a project stone dead"; some deception lets us get data we couldn't get otherwise
Evidence — Pruitt (2008)Escorts replied to a self-identified sociologist only 15% of the time, but to a "potential client" 60% of the time — some groups can't be studied openly
Rule — ASA guidelines (followed by the American Society of Criminology)Deception only if: not harmful; justified by the study's value; no equally good honest method; no more than minimal risk; and approved by an institutional review board (IRB)

11. Entering the field and keeping trust

Entering the field

  • Do background work first: know your research questions and your own attitude towards the people.
  • Learn their dress, routines and even language — Whyte learned Italian, which showed respect.
  • Build trust with at least one informant. Susan Miller got into the police through an open-minded police chief and two officer friends who vouched for her and arranged interviews.

Keeping relationships

  • Confidentiality is the cornerstone. Decker & Van Winkle refused to tell gang members what others had said and did not let members sit in on interviews — so they were seen as "solid".
  • Jody Miller (2000) started with easy, harmless survey questions (age, home, school) before moving to sensitive topics, and protected confidentiality.

Advice from Whyte and Wolcott

  1. Give a plausible, honest explanation of yourself and your study.
  2. Keep the support of key people in the group.
  3. Don't be too aggressive in questioning (e.g. about illegal activities); you are not the police.
  4. Don't fake being like them — a friendly interest is enough.
  5. Avoid giving or receiving money or gifts, but don't break normal give-and-take (a lift to the shop is fine).
  6. Be ready for tension if several groups are involved — it's hard not to take sides.
Getting in is about respect and a good "door-opener". Staying in is about keeping secrets, not judging, and not pretending.

12. Phenomenology

Phenomenology is a qualitative approach that explores how individuals experience and make sense of phenomena. It focuses on the subjective meanings people attach to their lived experiences, rather than objective explanations or cause-and-effect. Researchers try to capture "what it is like" to experience something (lecture slides).

Phenomenology asks "what does this feel like from the inside?" Not "how many prisoners are there?" or "what causes crime?", but "what is it like to be in prison?" or "what is it like to live in fear after a robbery?"

Methods of phenomenology: interviews · observations · personal narratives (life stories, diaries, written accounts).

Phenomenology asks…It does NOT ask…
What is it like to be a victim of acid violence?How many acid attacks happened last year?
How do released prisoners make sense of returning home?Does prison length cause reoffending?
How do women experience harassment on public transport?What percentage of women are harassed?
If an MCQ option says "cause-and-effect" or "objective measurement", it is not phenomenology.

13. Interviews

TypeQuestionsFlexibilityLike…
StructuredFixed, predetermined questions in a set orderVery limitedA job application form read aloud
Semi-structuredOpen-ended core questions + follow-up probes; can explore unexpected topics while staying focusedMediumA planned conversation with a guide
UnstructuredNo fixed questions or order; starts with a broad opening; the respondent guides the conversationVery high"Tell me your story…"

Intensive interviewing (book, p. 237) = open-ended, relatively unstructured questioning in which the interviewer seeks in-depth information on the interviewee's feelings, experiences and/or perceptions.

A probe is a follow-up question to get more detail: "Can you give me an example?", "What happened next?", "What do you mean by…?"

Same topic, three ways — police harassment of street vendors in Dhaka.
Structured: "Have you been asked for a bribe by police in the past month? Yes / No."
Semi-structured: "Can you describe your experiences with the police while selling?" → probe: "How did you respond?"
Unstructured: "Tell me about your life as a vendor."
Decker & Van Winkle's gang study shows the spirit of qualitative interviews: they wanted every gang member to "tell their story in their own words", using open-ended questions with probes rather than tick-box answers.

Class exercise: writing 10–12 semi-structured questions

  1. Start with easy, rapport-building questions.
  2. Group core questions by theme (experience, meaning, coping, help-seeking, solutions).
  3. Use open "how/what/describe" questions — no yes/no, no leading questions.
  4. Write one or two probes under each core question.
  5. End with "Is there anything else you'd like to tell me?"

14. Case study, and "Life in a Gang"

Case study research is an in-depth qualitative approach that examines a specific real-world phenomenon within its context. It analyses a single case or a small number of cases, often using multiple sources of evidence (lecture slides).

A case study zooms in on one thing — one gang, one police station, one village, one event — and looks at it from every angle (interviews, observation, documents) to understand it completely.

Limitations (four)

LimitationWhy
Limited generalizabilityOne or a few cases may not represent others
Potential for researcher biasThe researcher's closeness shapes what is noticed and reported
Time-consuming and resource-intensiveLong fieldwork with many sources of evidence
Difficulty in replicationThe case and the researcher's relationship to it are unique
"Inability to give in-depth understanding" is not a limitation — depth is the case study's main strength.

The book's case study: Life in a Gang (Decker & Van Winkle, 1996)

  • Gang definition: "an age-graded peer group that exhibits some permanence, engages in criminal activity, and has some symbolic representation of membership".
  • Who counted as a member: self-identification — the screening question "Are you claiming…?", checked with other members where possible.
  • Methods: a field ethnographer observed "on the streets" + intensive interviews with open-ended questions and probes.
  • Inductive: categories came from how the gang members themselves described their world, not from theory.

Thick description (Geertz, 1973) = a rich description that gives a sense of what it is like to be in a setting, from the participants' point of view. This is the goal of much qualitative research.

A "thin" description says "he winked". A "thick" description explains what the wink meant — a joke, a signal, a warning — to the people who were there.

15. Sampling people and events

TypeMeaningExample
Purposive samplingChoosing people on purpose because they can inform the research question — the usual qualitative methodInterviewing only women who use public buses daily
Snowball samplingEach participant recommends others — useful for hidden groupsDecker & Van Winkle: made 500+ contacts to reach a final sample of 99 gang members
Theoretical sampling (Glaser & Strauss, 1967)Choosing new settings or people step by step, as earlier findings show who is importantDecker & Van Winkle chased female, older and non-Black gang members to cover all types
Snowball sampling is like asking "Who else should I talk to?" at the end of every interview. Theoretical sampling is deciding who to talk to next based on what you have just learned.

One setting or many? Studying one setting gives a very detailed picture but makes it hard to generalize; studying more than one setting strengthens conclusions (King, Keohane & Verba, 1994).

18. Last-minute revision sheet

  1. Ethnography = immersion in participants' natural environment to understand behaviours, beliefs, practices from their perspective.
  2. Street Corner Society = Whyte, 1943; overt; Doc (key informant); ~4 years. Criticisms: illegal acts (vote fraud), Doc's influence/rivalry with Chick, anonymity.
  3. Overt = more ethical, easier communication, but reactive effect. Covert = more access, but less ethical and stressful.
  4. Phenomenology = lived experience, subjective meaning, "what it is like"; methods = interviews, observations, personal narratives.
  5. Interviews: structured (fixed) · semi-structured (core questions + probes) · unstructured (broad opening, respondent leads).
  6. Case study = in-depth study of a real-world phenomenon in context, few cases, multiple evidence. Limitations: generalizability, researcher bias, time/resources, replication.
  7. Continuum: complete observer (Miller) · participant-observer (Whyte, Venkatesh) · covert participant (Humphreys, Alfred, Goffman).
  8. Reactive effect = behaviour changes because of being observed. Going native = researcher absorbed into the group.
  9. Covert problems: no open notes · no suspicious questions · hard role · keep up the act.
  10. Sampling: purposive + snowball (Decker: 500+ → 99) + theoretical (Glaser & Strauss).
  11. Thick description (Geertz) and reflexive design (Maxwell).
  12. FGDs are not in this quiz.
